# Deep Elements - Sources

All factual claims in the game (element facts, supply-risk labels, device compositions,
mine locations, quiz answers) were reviewed against the references below, most recently in
**November 2026** (see "November 2026 review" below).
The gameplay itself is deliberate fiction - see the framing note in `TEACHERS_GUIDE.md`
("the facts are real, the mining is a game"). Factual corrections are treated as bugs;
please report any error you find.

## Principal references

**Element facts & supply-risk labels**
- USGS *Mineral Commodity Summaries* (2025/2026 editions) - production shares, reserves:
  https://www.usgs.gov/centers/national-minerals-information-center/mineral-commodity-summaries
- US DOI *Final 2025 List of Critical Minerals*:
  https://www.doi.gov/pressreleases/interior-department-releases-final-2025-list-of-critical-minerals
- EU *Critical Raw Materials Act* list (2023):
  https://www.consilium.europa.eu/en/infographics/critical-raw-materials/
- Royal Society of Chemistry element pages: https://www.rsc.org/periodic-table
- Discovery/synthetic-element history: ORNL (tennessine), Berkeley Lab (berkelium/californium),
  RIKEN (nihonium), NASA APXS documentation (curium on Mars rovers).

Supply-risk labels in the game are a coarse LOW/MEDIUM/HIGH/CRITICAL simplification of the
above lists (CRITICAL: supply dominated by one country or one by-product stream, with no
good substitute); where EU and US assessments differ, the more widely cited classification was used.
The official lists say only critical or not, and also weigh economic importance, so the
four steps are the game's own reading: zinc, zirconium, lead and potassium are on the US
2025 list but are LOW here, because many countries produce them.

**Device scenarios**
- Smartphone element counts: ACS/Compound Interest "Elements of a Smartphone" (~30 elements);
  counts of up to about 60 elements are quoted for electronic devices as a whole.
- EV motors & rare-earth magnet masses (about 1–2 kg typical; hybrid motors use less):
  Visual Capitalist / industry data; IEEE Spectrum.
- Satellite count (15,000+ active; 17,099 on 26 Oct 2026): live orbital catalogues
  (planet4589.org, orbitalradar.com).
- Blue LED history (bright blue LEDs from the early 1990s, Nobel 2014): IEEE Milestone "Practical Blue LED",
  https://ethw.org/Milestones:Practical_Blue_Light-Emitting_Diode,_1989%E2%80%931993
- Satellite "gold foil" is aluminium-coated plastic insulation, not gold - so the Satellite
  mission asks about gold in the electronics instead: NOAA NESDIS,
  https://www.nesdis.noaa.gov/news/good-gold-are-satellites-covered-gold-foil
- JWST beryllium mirrors & 48 g gold coating: NASA JWST documentation.
- MRI (Nb₃Sn/NbTi superconductors, helium cooling), fuel cells, jet-engine superalloys
  (incl. Re/Hf single-crystal alloys): standard engineering references
  (e.g. Rolls-Royce *The Jet Engine*).

**Sensitivity review (July 2026):** conflict-mineral and environmental content was
independently reviewed for a 10–14 classroom audience. Verdict: accurate and proportionate.
The cobalt entry scopes documented child labour to the artisanal (hand-dug) segment of DRC
supply (cf. Wilson Center / CFR analyses), and the Indonesian tin entry names the actual
mechanism (offshore dredging, Bangka-Belitung reef loss).

**Families and the shape of the table**
- IUPAC *Nomenclature of Inorganic Chemistry* (Red Book 2005), IR-3.5:
  https://iupac.org/wp-content/uploads/2016/07/Red_Book_2005.pdf
- IUPAC periodic table: https://iupac.org/what-we-do/periodic-table-of-elements/

The Element Runs use school names for the families. Group 12 (zinc, cadmium, mercury) is
counted with the transition metals, which the Red Book allows but not every chemist does;
the six metalloids are a convention; "other metals" and "other nonmetals" are the metals
and nonmetals outside every named family; lanthanum to lutetium are shown as the fifteen
lanthanides. "All 94 elements found in nature" counts technetium, promethium, astatine,
francium, neptunium and plutonium, which occur only as fleeting traces.

### Not verified

These could not be checked against an opened source and rest on general knowledge, search
summaries or a single secondary source. The wording was made cautious where that was possible.

- The main Royal Society of Chemistry site, Britannica, the IUPAC Gold Book, mindat, the
  NIH supplement pages, UNESCO and several company sites refused automated access, so
  nothing was checked against them; the glossary was judged without the Gold Book.
- Cards: borax on the Silk Road (boron); the selenium content of Brazil nuts; gallium's
  boiling point (handbooks give 2,204 to about 2,400 °C); lutetium as the hardest and
  densest lanthanide; gadolinium's Curie point near 20 °C; the yearly output of ruthenium
  and osmium; "about one gram" of berkelium ever made; the number of bohrium and hassium
  atoms made; the price of californium.
- Devices: germanium in silicon-germanium chips, the europium phosphor and palladium in
  fuel cells were not individually sourced; the titanium and zirconium oxides in mirror
  glass-ceramics rest on secondary sources; "up to 60 elements" in electronics is a widely
  repeated figure (a count of 69 also circulates).
- Mines: CBMM's share of world niobium (the caption now says "most"); San Rafael's rank
  among tin mines; Tanco's past rank as a tantalum source; whether indium is recovered at
  Neves-Corvo; that nobody enters the ore body at Cigar Lake.
- Most fun facts for the common elements, and most quiz questions, were passed from
  general knowledge without a lookup.

## What is not individually sourced

The following were written from general reference knowledge (principally the USGS
Mineral Commodity Summaries and the RSC element pages). They were read again in the
November 2026 review, but only some were checked against an opened source: the "reality check" line for each of the 94 elements found in nature, the one-line
