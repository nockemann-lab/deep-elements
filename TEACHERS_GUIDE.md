# Deep Elements - Teacher's Guide

*A mining puzzle game about the periodic table, everyday technology, and where materials
really come from.*

**Ages:** ~10–14 (usable to 16+ for supply-chain discussion) · **Session:** 15–45 min ·
**Platform:** any modern browser, keyboard or touch · **Cost:** free · **Accounts:** none ·
**Tracking:** none - the game stores progress only on the student's own device.
**Feedback:** have you used it with a class? Please tell us in the short, anonymous
teacher survey at the end of this guide.

*Content last reviewed: November 2026.*

---

## The one thing to know first

> **The facts are real. The mining is a game.**
> Every element fact, device recipe, supply-risk label and world-map mine location in Deep
> Elements is researched and sourced (see `SOURCES.md`). *How* you collect the elements -
> glowing crystals, falling boulders, vengeful snakes - is pure arcade fiction. Real mining
> involves considerably more paperwork and considerably fewer spiders.

This split is deliberate, and it is itself teachable: comparing a model with reality is a
core science skill. The **Game vs. Reality** table below is designed to be used directly as
a classroom exercise.

## Learning objectives

After playing, students can:

1. Name several chemical elements inside everyday devices (phone, battery, solar panel,
   wind turbine, MRI scanner…) and match elements to the component they enable.
2. Explain that elements are natural resources mined in specific places, and that some
   ("critical raw materials") carry supply risks because production concentrates in few
   countries.
3. Locate real mines on a world map and connect them to technologies they use daily.
4. Navigate the periodic table's structure: groups (alkali metals, halogens, noble gases,
   lanthanides…) share family behaviour - the *Element Runs* are literally built out of
   these groups: in the *Deep Element Run* each family has a hall of its own, and *The
   Table Run* lays its halls out as the columns of the table.
5. Distinguish the elements found in nature (Z 1–94, six of them only as fleeting traces)
   from synthetic, lab-made elements.
6. Critique a model: identify where the game simplifies reality and why.

## Curriculum connections

- **Chemistry:** periodic table structure and element families; properties and uses of
  metals, metalloids and nonmetals; natural vs. synthetic elements.
- **Geography:** global distribution of natural resources; resource-dependent economies;
  reading coordinates and world maps (Critical Missions mode).
- **Earth science / sustainability:** finite resources, mining impacts, recycling and
  "urban mining" (the game's gold fact - a drawer of old phones is richer ore than most
  gold mines - is a ready-made recycling hook).
- **Citizenship / ethics (older students):** critical raw materials, supply-chain
  concentration, conditions in artisanal mining regions.
- **Science practices:** evaluating the strengths and limitations of models (see table).

## Suggested classroom flow (one 45-minute lesson)

1. **Hook (5 min):** "How many chemical elements are in your phone?" Collect guesses.
   (Answer per the game: around 30 - some counts run to 60 or more.)
2. **Tutorial (10 min):** students play Tutorial levels 1–2 - movement, digging,
   collecting, reading the element fact cards, boulders and dynamite. Each lesson is a
   chain of small tasks; the hint banner states the rule and, on the line
   marked ▶, what to do next.
3. **Mission (15 min):** each student picks one mine on the world map (Critical Missions)
   and completes it. Ask them to note *which country they were "in"* and *one element fact*
   from the side panel. Mines are marked easy, medium or hard - steer first-time players
   to an easy one. The first time a student finds an element, the level clock holds for
   eight seconds (a 📖 appears on the timer) so the card can be read without losing time;
   the creatures do not wait, so reading is safest from a quiet corner. Anything missed
   can be read afterwards in the field notebook (see below).
4. **Discussion (10 min):** pool findings on the board - which countries came up? Which
   elements appeared in more than one device? Use discussion questions below.
5. **Model critique (5 min):** hand out the Game vs. Reality table with the right column
   covered; students propose what really happens before revealing.

**Exit ticket:** finishing a mission opens the field notebook with that mission's
elements starred. Ask each student to pick one starred element and write down what it
does in the device and where in the world it comes from - a two-minute check that the
reading happened.

**Homework / challenge options:** finish all 6 tutorials; complete an *Element Run*
(*The First Twenty* is a short sprint; the *Deep Element Run* and *The Table Run* each
cover all 94 elements found in nature, and completing a whole group puts time back on the
clock); write a "reality check" for one more game mechanic; design a level for a classmate
(see *Students as level designers* below).

## Game vs. Reality

| In the game | In reality |
|---|---|
| You pick up a pure glowing indium crystal. | Indium is never mined directly - it is recovered in trace amounts as a byproduct of zinc smelting. |
| One miner digs a phone's worth of elements in minutes. | A phone's 30 or so elements cross dozens of mines, refineries and factories on several continents over months. |
| Gold sits in the rock as a shiny nugget. | Typical gold ore contains a few *grams* per tonne - nuggets are rare finds; giant crushers and chemical processing are the reality. |
| Cobalt is a blue gem you grab barehanded. | Cobalt arrives as ore in hundred-ton truckloads and is refined industrially; much of it is a byproduct of copper and nickel mining. |
| The exit opens when you hold all the elements. | Mines produce one or a few commodities; no mine on Earth yields a whole device's ingredient list. |
| Helium is a pickup deep underground. | True-ish, surprisingly: helium is extracted from natural-gas fields underground - but as a gas, not a crystal. |
| Dynamite is placed by hand, and you run from its short fuse. | Modern blasting uses engineered explosives, electronic detonators and evacuation protocols - nobody outruns a fuse. |
| Snakes, bats and spiders hunt you through tunnels. | Wildlife encounters happen at surface mines, but the real underground dangers are rockfalls, gases, heat and machinery. |
| A Geiger counter reveals buried bonus elements. | Radiometric surveying is real for uranium/thorium exploration - but it finds radioactive elements, not silver or germanium. At Cigar Lake, the one uranium mine on the map, it finds what a real one would. |
| Synthetic elements hide in a vault. | Elements beyond Z 94 are made in nuclear reactors and particle accelerators. Americium sits in household smoke detectors; the heaviest are made a few atoms at a time and last seconds or less. |
| Green gas pockets suffocate you, and explode if a blast touches them. | Real, and historically deadly: methane ("firedamp") explosions and suffocating gases killed thousands of coal miners. Canaries were carried as living gas alarms into the 1980s; today electronic sensors do the job. Real firedamp is invisible and has no smell, and a filter mask is no help where the air holds too little oxygen. |
| Bats hunt by sight, ice defeats a pickaxe, and basalt crumbles by itself. | Game rules, not science: cave bats find their way by echolocation, ice is far softer than rock, and basalt is a hard rock that is crushed for roads and railway beds. |
| A refuge chamber saves your progress. | Refuge chambers are real - sealed rooms stocked with air, water and food where trapped miners wait for rescue. They do not, sadly, undo accidents. |
| A water pump drains a flooded gallery in seconds. | Flooding has always been a central problem of deep mining. The first practical steam engines (Newcomen, 1712) were built to pump water out of mines - the game's pump is the ancestor of the Industrial Revolution. |
| A tunnel boring machine grinds back and forth through the level. | TBMs are real and enormous - each Gotthard machine was over 400 m long with its trailing gear - but they bore one tunnel, forwards, at 10 to 20 metres on a typical day. They do not patrol. |
| Every element sits in the ground as its own crystal. | Most elements are locked in minerals with other elements (aluminium in bauxite, titanium in ilmenite) and must be separated chemically. Several in the game - technetium, promethium, astatine, francium, neptunium, plutonium - occur in nature only as vanishing traces and are in practice made in reactors or particle accelerators. |

## Discussion questions

1. Which element surprised you most, and in which device does it hide?
2. The missions visit mines in the DR Congo, Chile and China, among other countries. What
   does it mean for the rest of the world when one country produces most of a critical
   material?
3. Why might a country's "supply risk" label change over a decade?
4. Your old phone contains gold, cobalt and rare earths. What *should* happen to it -
   and what usually does?
5. The game shows mining as one person with a pickaxe. Who is actually involved in
   getting cobalt from the ground into a battery?
6. In the *Deep Element Run* each hall holds one family of elements. Where does each
   family sit in the periodic table?
7. Why do the noble gases sit behind locked doors in the *Deep Element Run*? What real
   property is that a metaphor for?
8. If you could add one "reality check" to the game, what would it be?
9. Miners once carried canaries underground. What were the birds for - and what does the
   game's gas mechanic get right and wrong about mine gas?
10. Francium's card says "RADIOACTIVE TRACE" where most cards show a supply risk. Why
    would a supply-risk label make no sense for francium?
11. Step on a wrong answer pad and the game tells you what that element is really for.
    Pick one wrong answer you met: why was it a tempting choice?
12. In Endless Depths, silicon and iron turn up far more often than gold. What does that
    tell you about Earth's crust - and about why gold is expensive?

## Where the device recipes simplify

Each mission asks for nine elements and sorts them into device components. Every element
is really used in that kind of device, and the component assignments were reviewed in
October and November 2026 (see `SOURCES.md`). Three simplifications remain by design:

- **Solar Panel, Hydrogen Fuel Cell and Space Telescope** each combine several real
  technologies into one device - silicon cells alongside cadmium-telluride thin film;
  platinum catalysts alongside the nickel and zirconia of other fuel-cell types; infrared
  lenses alongside the molybdenum-silicon mirrors of extreme-ultraviolet telescopes. No
  single product contains all nine elements.
- **Supply-risk labels** are a coarse four-step reading (low, medium, high, critical) of
  the US and EU critical minerals lists. *Critical* is the highest step. The game keeps it
  for ten elements - cobalt, gallium, niobium, neodymium, europium, terbium, dysprosium,
  rhenium, rhodium and iridium - where most of the supply comes from one country, or only
  as a by-product of another metal, and industry has no good substitute. Elements that are
  not mined commodities carry a different label instead: *REACTOR-MADE* (technetium,
  promethium, neptunium, plutonium) or *RADIOACTIVE TRACE* (polonium, astatine, radon,
  francium, radium, actinium, protactinium).
- **Element families** in the *Deep Element Run* and the notebook follow common school
  usage, which differs in places from IUPAC's recommendations. *Transition metals* here
  means groups 3–12; IUPAC notes that group 12 (zinc, cadmium, mercury) is not always
  included. The six *metalloids* are a convention with no official definition. *Other
  metals* and *other nonmetals* are simply the metals and nonmetals that belong to no
  named family (the first are often called post-transition or p-block metals). IUPAC
  prefers *lanthanoids* and *actinoids* to the familiar *lanthanides* and *actinides*, and
  its *rare earths* are the lanthanoids plus scandium and yttrium. Elements 104–118 are
  coloured by their place in the table: the chemistry of most has never been measured.

These make good extension tasks: "find one thing in this recipe that a real engineer
would object to."

## The quiz in each mission

Every mission contains one question, written on the cave wall beside a row of answer
pads marked with element symbols. Stepping on the right pad opens a sealed door - usually
the way to the exit, so the quiz is part of finishing the mission.

- **The question is shown twice.** It is written on the cave wall, and when a student
  walks up to the pads it also appears in the hint banner - under the mine on a computer,
  over it on a phone or tablet - so it cannot be missed.
- **The answer has been met.** In nearly every mission the answer is an element collected
  earlier in that same level, so the quiz is recall of a card they have seen, not a guess.
- **Wrong answers cost time, not the level.** A wrong pad takes thirty seconds off the
  clock and says what that element is really used for. Guessing through every pad is
  therefore possible but expensive - one wrong pad usually costs a star - and nobody loses
  a level to one mistake.
- **The pads change places.** The answers are dealt onto the pads afresh every time a
  mission loads, so "it is the top-left one" cannot be remembered or passed along.

The *Element Runs* work the same way but have several doors each. A door asks about the
group of elements the player has just crossed, and opens only its own door.

**Answer key.** Every question in the game, with its answer and the other pads on offer,
is listed in `ANSWER_KEY.md`, which comes with the game's files. It is written from the
level files themselves, so it always matches the game, and it is kept out of the game's
own screens so that it stays with the teacher.

A useful follow-up question in class: "Which wrong pad did you step on, and why was it a
tempting answer?"

## Help built into the element cards

- **"In this device" line.** On a mission, each card opens with what that element actually
  does in the device being built - for example, indium in a smartphone is "the clear,
  conductive coating that senses your touch".
- **Underlined words.** Technical words such as *cathode*, *alloy*, *isotope* and *ore* are
  dotted-underlined the first time they appear on a card. Pointing at one, or tapping it
  on a touch screen, shows a one-sentence meaning.
- **Supply-risk stickers.** The Field Guide on the main menu explains the four levels, and
  the notebook explains where the labels come from. Pointing at a sticker shows what that
  level means.
- **The real mine.** Each mission opens with a line about the real mine it is set in.

## The field notebook

Every element a student collects, in any mode, is recorded in the
**Field notebook**: a periodic table that fills in with a sticker for each find. Selecting
a sticker shows that element's page - what it is used for, a *reality check* on how it is
really obtained (every element found in nature has one), a fun fact, and where it was found.

The notebook opens by itself when a level is completed, with that level's finds starred.
It can also be opened from the main menu and the world map, and from the screen shown
after a failed attempt once something has been collected.

Classroom uses:

- **Reading without the clock.** Students who rushed past a card during play can read it
  afterwards, with no timer running.
- **A task that can be checked.** "Fill in ten notebook pages and write down one reality
  check" is easy to set and easy to verify on the student's screen before they close
  the game.
- **Table structure.** The notebook is coloured by element family, so a half-filled page
  shows at a glance which families a student has and has not met.

**The notebook lasts for one sitting.** It starts empty each time the game is opened, so
it always shows exactly what that student has found since then, and the next student on a
shared computer starts with a blank one. Closing or reloading the page empties it, so
students should note down or screenshot anything they need before they finish. *Tear out
every page and start a fresh notebook*, at the bottom of the notebook, empties it without
reloading.

## Students as level designers

The game comes with a level editor (`Level Editor.html`, in the same folder as the game;
the *Play a shared level* box on the main menu links to it). Students paint a mine, choose
the device it builds, place its elements and write a quiz question of their own;
**Validate** checks that the level can be finished. **Copy JSON** then gives the level as
text, which a classmate pastes into *Play a shared level*. Writing a fair question with
tempting wrong answers is a good test of what a student has understood. The editor needs
a mouse and a large screen.

## Context notes for teachers (two missions with heavy history)

Two of the mines carry heavy human history that a one-line map
caption cannot do justice - students may find it when they search, so be prepared:

- **Cerro Rico (Potosí, Bolivia):** the in-game caption is deliberately spare. The full
  story includes the Spanish colonial *mita* forced-labour system, a historic death toll
  so large the mountain is remembered as "the mountain that eats men", and difficult
  present-day conditions - ten thousand or more miners still work it, and the summit is
  so honeycombed that UNESCO lists the site as endangered. Rich material for an older
  class discussion on colonialism and resource wealth.
- **Grasberg (Papua, Indonesia):** the game uses a neutral geological framing. The mine
  is also associated with long-running indigenous land-rights and environmental
  controversies (riverine tailings disposal). Its neighbouring tropical glaciers - the
  last between the Himalayas and the Andes - are nearly gone due to climate change, which
  is itself a teachable fact.

## Practical & accessibility notes

- Works offline once loaded; no login, no ads, no data collection - safe for school networks.
  The only exception is the optional teacher survey at the end of this guide, which loads
  from Microsoft Forms when a teacher chooses to open it.
- Fully keyboard-playable: arrow keys or WASD to move, 1–5 to use tools, P to pause,
  R to restart (R asks for confirmation, so a stray keypress does not lose a level).
- On tablets and phones the miner is steered with an on-screen D-pad, or by dragging a
  finger across the mine; holding either keeps the miner walking at keyboard pace. Tools
  are used by tapping them. A keyboard is still easier in the tight spots of the hard
  mines, so prefer keyboards where you have them.
- Element identification never relies on colour alone - every element shows its chemical
  symbol; the periodic-table tracker labels cells with symbols.
- *Effects: Full / Reduced* on the main menu is for motion- and flash-sensitive players.
  *Reduced* removes the screen shake, the flashes of explosions and of the screen shown
  when the miner is lost, and the looping animations. A device set to "reduce motion"
  starts in *Reduced*.
- Difficulty on the world map is shown by colour *and* by pips under each marker (one,
  two or three dots for easy, medium, hard), so it does not depend on colour vision.
- Reading load is bite-sized (1–2 sentence fact cards). The clock holds for eight seconds
  on each newly found element, and the pause key (P) lets slower readers take longer. Hint
  banners stay for at least fifteen seconds and are copied into the event log, so a missed
  hint can be read again.
- In the six tutorial lessons the mine waits for its reader: when a hint or the card of a
  newly found element appears and the miner stands still, creatures, machines, water and
  the clock all stop (the banner shows "⏸ The mine waits while you read") until the text
  has had time to be read or the miner moves on. A pupil who keeps walking is not held up.
  Missions and runs do not wait.
- A lesson need not be started again after a mistake. Most parts of a lesson are saved as
  their task appears: after a mishap the card offers "↩️ TRY THIS PART AGAIN", and a pupil
  who has used up a tool or blocked the way can pause (P) and choose "↩️ RETRY THIS PART".
  Neither costs points. Lesson 6 saves in its refuge chambers instead, as the hard
  missions do, and going back to one costs 100 points.
